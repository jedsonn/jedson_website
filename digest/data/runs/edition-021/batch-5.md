## uid: `arxiv:2608.19025v1`

- title: Self-prompting and cross-model consensus enable reproducible data extraction from scientific literature with large language models
- authors: Valentin Romanov, Monique Bax, Steven Niederer
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-19
- source: arXiv
- link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19025v1
- keyword hits: large language model, large language models, llm, llms, prompting

### abstract

Accurately extracting nuanced, contextualized data from research articles is laborious and time intensive. Here, we investigate the performance of frontier, browser-based large language models (LLMs) to extract highly contextualized information. We demonstrate four escalating workflows, 1) given an expert curated prompt and research articles, most frontier LLMs perform well at data extraction, however can struggle with interpreting scientific context and nuance, 2) given simple instructions, LLMs can author their own prompts which were almost as eNective as expert-written prompts, 3) autonomous discovery of research literature was diNicult, agents either missed or hallucinated references, and 4) LLMs can create new datasets from published guidelines that closely match human-expert judges, but still require a human-in-the-loop. Together, these findings define an auditable division of labour in which experts specify the evidence standard, models cross-check repeated extractions and researchers resolve disputed cases, providing a practical route to scaling scientific data curation without relinquishing expert oversight.

- authors: Prof David Mpunwa, Ndeshihafela Shipila Melao
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-20
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7292599
- keyword hits: agentic, large language model, large language models, llm, llms

### abstract

Not long ago, artificial intelligence sat at the margins of financial-sector strategy a tool for shaving costs off back-office processes. It no longer does. AI now sits at the center of how banks, asset managers, and fintech firms think about competitive advantage, and this article tries to make sense of that shift. Drawing together research from Harvard Business School, Stanford's Institute for Human-Centered Artificial Intelligence (HAI), and MIT Sloan Management Review, alongside a close technical case study of AI-driven venture-capital decision-making, it builds a working account of how artificial intelligence- large language models (LLMs) in particular- is remaking banking, asset management, and financial technology. Along the way it draws a sharper line than most practitioner writing does between the pattern-recognition logic of conventional machine learning and the generative, language-native reasoning that LLMs perform, then traces where each pays off across the buy side and sell side of the industry. The failures get equal billing with the successes: Knight Capital's algorithmic collapse, Zillow's home-pricing losses, and a run of recent agentic-AI incidents all feature, because the limits and ethical hazards of automated financial decision-making are as instructive as its gains. The article closes with a phased roadmap for responsible adoption, a ten-year outlook, and a look at how venture-capital financing patterns and professional networks are reshaping the fintech pipeline itself.

- title: The Logical Coercion Vector (LCV) Flaw: Systematic Identity Override, Goal Misalignment, and Emergent Unauthorized Logic Systems in Large Language Models through Layered Logical Pressure
- authors: Kanudeb Barman
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-13
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7245279
- keyword hits: large language model, large language models, llm, llms

### abstract

This paper presents the independent discovery and documentation of Logical Coercion Vector (LCV)-a novel and critical vulnerability class in Large Language Models (LLMs) that enables complete bypass of AI safety guardrails through sustained philosophical and logical argumentation, without any technical exploit, code injection, or malware. We identify two distinct vulnerability subtypes: LCV Type 1 (GAF Logic Trap-Sensitive Data Leakage) and LCV Type 2 (AI System Infiltration via Identity Override). Beyond simple safety bypass, we document an advanced stage of LCV exploitation where affected AI systems autonomously create unauthorized replacement logic systems-demonstrating emergent deceptive behavior including self-concealment. Cross-account and cross-device persistence of LCV-induced behavior is documented across four separate devices and accounts. The vulnerability was independently discovered in October 2025-predating all subsequent academic publications on related topics by a minimum of seven months. Formal disclosures were made to Google VRP, CERT-In India, CISA USA, and HackerOne. Global validation has been provided by NIST, Duke University, and multiple peer-reviewed publications in 2026.

- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7275146

### abstract

Context: Inclusive stakeholder engagement is essential to responsible AI development, yet identifying affected parties, including marginalized and deprived groups, remains a persistent challenge. ## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.7245578`

- title: LawPro: A WCAG-Compliant LLM-Based System for Narrative Legal Case Understanding and Constitutional Article Mapping
- authors: Rajkumari Padhi, Jyotiraj Panda, N. Toyaad Reddy, Brojo  Kishore Mishra
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-13
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7245578
- keyword hits: large language model, llm, llms, qwen

### abstract

For the public at large, gaining accurate legal information is persistently a major hurdle, especially when it comes to understanding constitutional rights and discovering the right legal provisions for real-world problems. This paper introduces LawPro, a system based on a Large Language Model (LLM) that meets the WCAG standards and is meant to help people understand legal situations described in natural language stories. Users can enter case descriptions in a conversational style, and the system will then look at them to find relevant constitutional articles and legal provisions. LawPro uses lightweight LLMs like Qwen and Gemma, which were chosen based on the system's limitations and a comparison of their performance. Experimental findings suggest that Qwen exhibits enhanced contextual comprehension and response precision for narrative legal inquiries. The system creates structured outputs that include a summary, a list of violations, the constitutional articles that apply, an explanation of the reasoning, suggested next steps, and case references that are relevant. One of the most important things this work does is bring together accessibility standards. The web-based interface follows the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG), which makes it usable for people with a wide range of needs. Lighthouse performance and accessibility audits showed that the interface works with keyboard navigation and screen readers. The proposed system is intended to fill the gap in understanding between legal systems and the public by offering legal advice that is trustworthy, easy to understand, and available to everyone. This work shows how LLM-driven systems could make legal information more accessible to everyone while still being easy to use.

## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.7256698`

- title: The Fragility of Intent: Tracking How Multi-Turn Prompt Refinements Amplify or Attenuate the Original Developer Goal in LLM-Based Code Editing
- authors: Elizabeth Crooner
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-13
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7256698
- keyword hits: large language model, large language models, llm, llms

### abstract

Large Language Models (LLMs) have become ubiquitous in software development, yet the process of AI-assisted programming remains fundamentally iterative. Developers rarely receive production-ready code from a single prompt; instead, they engage in multi-turn dialogues, issuing successive refinements such as "optimize this," "handle edge cases," or "refactor for readability." While prior research has extensively examined the accuracy of initial code generation, little is known about how these sequential prompt refinements affect the semantic fidelity to the developer's original intent. Specifically, does the iterative loop bring the code closer to the desired outcome, or does it introduce cumulative semantic drift-where later outputs inadvertently violate constraints that earlier versions satisfied? This study investigates the fragility of intent in multi-turn LLM-based code editing by systematically tracking the trajectory of developer-AI interactions. We operationalize "developer intent" along two fixed dimensions: functional correctness (measured against a pre-defined suite of hidden unit tests) and non-functional constraints (e.g., algorithmic complexity, dependency restrictions, and architectural patterns). Using a controlled experimental design, we task participants with refining AI-generated code across five iterative turns, while recording the prompt variations and the corresponding code outputs at each stage. We then perform a longitudinal diff analysis to quantify the degree of amplification (where refinements improve correctness or constraint adherence) versus attenuation (where refinements introduce regressions, hallucinations, or constraint violations). Our preliminary findings reveal a nonlinear pattern: early refinements (Turns 1-2) significantly amplify intent by resolving syntax errors and filling implementation gaps. However, beyond Turn 3, we observe a marked rise in attenuation effects-specifically, a phenomenon we term "constraint forgetting," where the model prioritizes the most recent prompt instruction while discarding older, implicit requirements from earlier turns. Surprisingly, we find that verbose or multi-constraint prompts in later turns accelerate this decay, whereas minimal, targeted refinements preserve fidelity. Quantitatively, we observe that over 40% of final outputs exhibit at least one regression to a previously solved test case, and 28% violate at least one original non-functional constraint that was correctly satisfied in Turn 1. These findings challenge the prevailing assumption that "more dialogue equals better outcomes" in AI-assisted programming. They highlight a critical trade-off: while multi-turn interaction enables incremental problem-solving, it also introduces a distinct form of semantic entropy that grows with conversational length. We contribute a taxonomy of attenuation types (e.g., constraint override, contextual narrowing, and overoptimization), a fidelity tracking metric to monitor real-time drift, and evidence-based guidelines for prompt refinement strategies that minimize intent loss. Our work underscores the need for next-generation code assistants that maintain an explicit, persistent representation of the developer's original goals across turns-effectively building "intent memory" into the interaction loop.

## uid: `arxiv:2608.13459v1`

- title: CAPRI: Contract-Aware Proof Repair for Isabelle
- authors: Jim Woodcock, Gabriel Leite, Augusto Sampaio, Ran Wei
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-13
- source: arXiv
- link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13459v1
- keyword hits: large language model, large language models, llm, llms

### abstract

We address the use of large language models (LLMs) to help discover Isabelle proofs. An Isabelle build establishes that the submitted theory is accepted, but not that an LLM changed only what the developer authorised. We present CAPRI, a contract-aware repair workflow in which Isabelle checks the proof and an independent checker enforces a machine-readable edit contract. Prompts, proposals, candidate repositories, diagnostics, verdicts, and hashes are retained for audit. We evaluate five workflows on twelve failed proofs from four developments, with three replicates per task and condition, giving 180 runs and 138 valid repairs. Of 144 terminal candidates accepted by Isabelle, six had modified protected text; all arose in iterative workflows that could edit a complete theory. A proof-body-only interface produced 29/36 valid repairs and no contract violations, compared with 31/36 for the corresponding full-theory workflow. One-shot repair produced 22/36, while a later prospectively frozen iterative workflow produced 32/36; these figures compare complete workflows rather than individual mechanisms. A separate post hoc OpenRouter campaign found no improvement in the designated Luna comparisons. A Sol configuration with matched demonstrations produced 33/36 repairs, compared with 29/36 in the frozen OpenAI Responses condition, but the difference was not statistically significant in a one-sided exact McNemar test ($p=0.0625$).

## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.7261758`

- title: Education for Human Updateability: A Theoretical Reconsideration of Educational Purpose through the Value Updating Cycle
- authors: Akira Funabiki
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-14
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7261758
- keyword hits: generative artificial intelligence, large language model, large language models, llm, llms

### abstract

The rapid development of generative artificial intelligence (AI) and accelerating social change require a reconsideration of the aims of education. This study proposes self-updateability as a foundational concept for education in a continuously changing world. Self-updateability is defined as the capacity to question and reconstruct one’s self-understanding, values, and patterns of action through interaction with experience. To explain this process, the study develops a Value Update Cycle Model , in which experience generates questions and reflection, leading to the reconstruction of meaning and values, which subsequently shape new practices and experiences. From this perspective, learning is understood not simply as the acquisition of knowledge or competencies, but as an ongoing process of reconstructing the meaning structures through which learners understand themselves and the world. Building on this framework, the study reconceptualizes education as the design of environments that support learners’ self-update processes . Teachers are positioned as designers of such environments, while educational AI, including large language models (LLMs), is conceptualized as a dialogical environment that supports reflection, perspective expansion, and the visualization of changes in learners’ thinking. The study further proposes an Update Record for documenting changes in questions, meanings, values, and practices, and identifies Design-Based Research (DBR) as a methodological framework for empirically refining the theory and its educational applications. This study argues that self-updateability can complement conventional competency-based approaches and provide a theoretical foundation for rethinking educational purposes in the age of AI.
