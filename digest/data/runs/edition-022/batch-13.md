## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.7311259`

- title: Measuring Audit Oversight Activities with Large Language Models: Evidence from Japan
- authors: Kenichi Yazawa, Kento Yamamoto
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-20
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7311259
- keyword hits: gpt-4, large language model, large language models

### abstract

The activities of audit-oversight bodies, most notably audit committees in U.S. and U.K. firms, are central to the reliability of financial reporting. Evidence on them, however, rests largely on inputs and structural attributes (independence, expertise, meeting frequency, governance form), which capture the capacity for oversight and leave the substance of what these bodies do unobserved. We address this gap in Japan, where annual securities reports have been required since 2019 to describe those activities in narrative form. Applying a large language model (GPT-4o) to read that narrative in context, we construct an Audit Oversight Score (AOS) that grades reported oversight activity at scale, separating specific and evidenced activity from boilerplate instead of counting words, and split it into the body's own activity (ACT) and its coordination with the external auditor (COORD). The measure agrees with human coders as closely as two human coders agree with each other, and is reproduced by three other models. In an FY2019-FY2024 panel, substantive differences persist across firms and are not explained by the governance structure a firm has chosen. Firms scoring higher pay higher audit fees, consistent with oversight and the external audit being complements. During the COVID-19 pandemic, when incentives to misreport rose, a higher ACT is associated with smaller absolute discretionary accruals even within the same firm. Both associations run through ACT rather than COORD, an asymmetry visible only once oversight is measured along two dimensions.

## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.7299990`

- title: Exploring Dependence, Overreliance, and Addiction Related Behaviors Associated with Large Language Model Use Among Software Engineers
- authors: Ronnie de Souza Santos, Italo Santos, Matheus de Morais Leça, Cleyton Magalhaes, Mairieli Wessel
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-20
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7299990
- keyword hits: large language model, large language models, llm, llms

### abstract

Context: The widespread adoption of Large Language Models has changed how software engineers perform everyday development activities. While these systems provide substantial support for tasks such as code generation, debugging, and documentation, their use has raised questions regarding developers' reliance on them and the potential emergence of dependence, overreliance, and addiction related behaviors. Objective: This study investigates how software engineers experience the use of LLMs during professional software development, focusing on patterns of reliance and potentially problematic use. Method: An exploratory survey was conducted with 119 software practitioners around the world. The data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and qualitative thematic analysis of participants' open ended responses. Results: Software professionals have primarily experienced functional dependence, with LLMs becoming integrated into routine software engineering activities because of the productivity and efficiency they provide. Their responses also suggested patterns consistent with overreliance, particularly through prioritizing LLMs over documentation or peer consultation while continuing to verify generated outputs. Reports associated with addiction related behaviors were less common and mainly reflected difficulty moderating use of the technology rather than impaired control. Conclusion: Our study demonstrates that, as LLMs become a habitual component of professional software engineering practice, most reported use appears functional rather than problematic. Still, our findings indicate the importance of promoting appropriate reliance by supporting trust calibration, professional judgment, and verification throughout software development.

## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.7309400`

- title: The First Draft Problem: Productivity, Quality, and Physiological Strain in LLM-Assisted Writing
- authors: Xufei Liu, Hummy Song, Christian Terwiesch
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-20
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7309400
- keyword hits: large language model, large language models, llm, llms

### abstract

The use of large language models (LLMs) has grown rapidly over the past few years, especially in health care, where documentation burden remains a persistent problem for practicing clinicians. However, despite the deployment of many ambient scribes for documentation, it is still unclear how LLMs impact those who work with them. Across two preregistered studies, we examine how this shift-from writing documentation to editing an LLM-generated draft-impacts workers. We study this "first draft problem": eliminating the blank page does not remove the work of documentation but relocates it into revising and editing. In Study 1, an online experiment with 16 clinicians producing documentation following a teletherapy patient encounter, we establish the puzzle: counter to our hypotheses, editing an LLM-generated draft of the documentation as opposed to writing it from scratch does not result in time savings or quality improvements on average. Instead, while some clinicians experience no time savings but large improvements in quality after making substantial revisions, others experience meaningful time savings and no change in quality after making limited revisions. In other words, we observe significant heterogeneity in how clinicians engage with the same draft, which leads to variation in productivity and quality. In Study 2, a lab experiment with 100 university students summarizing video interviews, we unpack the mechanisms underlying this heterogeneity using granular keystroke logs, validated self-reports of workload, and micro-level physiological and eye-tracking measurements. In this setting, we find the expected productivity improvements: editing reduces task completion time by roughly 70%, raises quality, and lowers self-reported workload. Yet the physiological evidence tells a more nuanced story. Physiological markers indicate that fatigue accumulates faster when editing an LLM-generated draft than when writing from scratch. Thus, we find that (a) time savings can be real, but are setting dependent; (b) quality improvements are possible, but the relationship between how heavily a draft is revised and the quality of the result differs sharply across our two settings; and (c) while editing an LLM draft lowers reported cognitive load, the body still tires faster over time.

- title: Artificial Eloquence, or The Appearance of Good Writing
- authors: Megan Boyd
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-20
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7315224
- keyword hits: chatgpt, claude, gemini, generative ai

### abstract

This article examines the recurring stylistic habits of generative AI in legal writing. Although tools such as ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini differ in their capabilities and tendencies, they share certain habits that stem from the way generative AI produces text. It identifies those habits and contrasts them with the choices found in Supreme Court briefs written by leading appellate advocates. Those briefs show skilled writers varying sentence structure, controlling emphasis, selecting among available examples, and developing an argument rather than simply restating it. GenAI can reproduce many of the techniques associated with strong legal writing, but it applies them without the judgment necessary to make those techniques work. The distinction matters because it gives lawyers a way to identify writing whose polished and authoritative surface may warrant closer scrutiny, regardless of who or what produced it.

## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.7321287`

- title: Perspectivist Stance Detection in Spanish Civic Discourse: Soft-Label Training and Generative LLMs as Synthetic Annotators
- authors: María Teresa Muñoz Martín, Manuel Carlos Díaz Galiano, Eugenio Martínez Cámara
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-21
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7321287
- keyword hits: llama, llm, llms

### abstract

Stance detection remains critically under-resourced for Spanish, and existing corpora typically collapse multiple annotator judgments into a single majority label, discarding information about genuine linguistic and political ambiguity. To close this gap, we introduce XX-corpus (name withheld for double-blind review), a perspectivist stance detection corpus for Spanish civic discourse: 3,000 citizen comments from the Decide Madrid participatory platform across 387 policy proposals, each independently annotated by three of 180 native Spanish speakers. Rather than collapsing these three judgments into a single label, we preserve them all, making XX-corpus, to our knowledge, the only publicly available stance corpus that combines a civic-participation domain, hundreds of distinct policy targets, and individual annotator judgments with soft-label distributions. A model can learn to associate a policy proposal's own wording with its stance rather than genuinely interpreting the comment, a failure mode known as target leakage; we guard against it by evaluating encoder-based models and generative LLMs under a strict cross-topic protocol, in which no proposal seen during training or development reappears in the test set. Under this protocol, our best model, a QLoRA-tuned Llama-3.1-8B, reaches an F1 macro of 0.6541. We further ask whether preserving annotator disagreement pays off beyond benchmark scores, comparing hard-label training against soft-label training on the same encoder: soft-label training improves epistemic alignment with human judgment, reducing KL divergence by 25% and raising accuracy by 5.7 percentage points, without degrading hard-label performance, a gain corroborated by an independent, distance-based metric. Finally, we test whether this alignment requires retraining at all by sampling a generative LLM repeatedly instead: the resulting distribution recovers a comparable alignment without ever training on a human judgment distribution, and a bootstrap analysis confirms the LLM is statistically indistinguishable from a fourth human annotator.

## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.7316209`

- title: Making AI Tutoring Productive: Evidence from a Mastery-Based Math Practice Experiment
- authors: Philip Oreopoulos, Michael Liut, Alp Sungu, Nina Low
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-21
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7316209
- keyword hits: large language model, large language models, llm, llms

### abstract

Large language models (LLMs) can make tutoring more scalable, but only if students use them to reason through mistakes rather than avoid effort. We study this question in a randomized field experiment with more than 6, 000 middle-school students in Hamilton County Schools using NUMI, a research-based computer-assisted learning (CAL) platform. Students were randomized to AI versus CAL-only support, mastery versus non-mastery progression, and one of two math topics. One week later, they completed a delayed assessment covering practiced and unpracticed material. AI students progressed more slowly and attempted fewer questions, but answered more accurately conditional on reaching an attempt. The clearest mechanism appeared after mistakes: AI improved next-attempt correctness and reduced attempts needed to return to a correct answer, while increasing time spent on each question receiving structured support. Mastery increased three-correct-in-a-row attainment but did not by itself improve delayed learning. The most encouraging delayed-test evidence appears when AI is embedded in the mastery workflow, with marginally significant gains concentrated on practiced Exercise 1 material. The results suggest that LLM tutoring can add value over standard CAL, but its value depends on structure that turns mistakes into productive learning moments. ## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.7320742`

- title: Policy Learning in Language-Mediated Stochastic Systems
- authors: Yuan Cao, Siyang Gao, David Simchi-Levi
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-21
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7320742
- keyword hits: large language model, large language models, llm, llms

### abstract

The use of large language models (LLMs) makes the content of operational messages available to stochastic simulation, which enables the evaluation of communication policies that govern message timing, content, and subsequent receiver actions. Since repeated LLM calls are expensive, scalable simulation must group similar situations and reuse language outcomes collected for each group. This reuse creates an endogenous input problem, in which a stored language law changes simulated actions and future contexts, while those contexts define the law that the simulator should use. In this research, we formulate this relation through a policy-specific language input operator. We characterize exact reuse across policies and show that a precisely estimated shared input can create unavoidable error in operating values and reverse a dynamic policy ranking. Then, a decision-weighted analysis is developed, which studies representation, reference-to-policy context transport, and reference-to-stored adjustment components and measures their amplification through the operating system. We show that this amplification is generally unavoidable. We also construct a reusable empirical operator from one language sample and establish policy selection guarantees under a total call budget and an information lower bound for adaptive language collection. Numerical experiments on generated handoffs and code review conversations illustrate the practical importance of the proposed modeling and learning framework.

- posted: 2026-08-15
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7264842

### abstract

The research paper proposes a Synthetic Ethical Dialectics Engine (SEDE), a workflow driven AI governance framework that coordinates ethical debates among AI agents. Each debate stems from a distinct philosophical and technical foundation (utilitarian, deontological, and virtue ethics). The novel workflow integrates these moral philosophies using a reflective equilibrium approach. The proposed engine leverages LlaMa3.2:1b LLM within n8n development toolkit. The subordinate nodes within the workflow are implemented in Ja-vaScript to enable structured initial analyses, critical conflict analysis, and revised ethical judgments. We demonstrate how moral reasoning benefits a multiagent system as it brings forth diverse ethical perspectives. SEDE's outcome is sent in the form of a message to a human moderator for final review via the Telegram app. Thus, the paper discloses the implementation of ethical dialectics in the multi-agent system and further discusses its role for AI safety, policy simulation, and explainable AI ethics.
