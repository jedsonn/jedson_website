## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.7323940`

- title: The Open-Weight Visual Reasoning Turn: DeepSeek’s Multimodal Models and the Future of Visual Communication Practice,Technical Note / Opinion Paper
- authors: nashwa ali
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-21
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7323940
- keyword hits: deepseek

### abstract

Visual AI has moved past making convincing images. It can now look at a poster and say what works, what fails, and what to change. That shift matters for visual communication. DeepSeek’s open-weight models—VL2, Janus-Pro, OCR, and R1—can describe visual hierarchy, critique layouts, read old documents, and reason about storyboards. Existing metrics like FID and CLIP measure fidelity, not whether a design communicates. I propose a simple fix: a Visual Collaboration Disclosure (VCD) that documents how AI contributed and how humans made the final call. The argument is grounded in four example workflows: design critique, historical document analysis, storyboard planning, and accessibility auditing. The risks are real—homogenization, loss of craft, misuse—but they are manageable if we treat AI as a collaborator and disclose its role. Visual communication can lead this shift, or it can chase it. I argue it should lead.

## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.7318642`

- title: GenAI Assessment and Language Equity: Drawing the Line between Support and Substitution
- authors: Grace Li
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-21
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7318642
- keyword hits: generative artificial intelligence

### abstract

Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) has changed higher education assessment by collapsing the practical boundary between language assistance and substantive authorship. For students who use English as an additional language (EAL), this collapse is not neutral. When integrity rules treat GenAI as a single category of unauthorised assistance, they risk converting linguistic disadvantage into integrity risk – the students most reliant on language support become the students most exposed to suspicion, inconsistent enforcement, and sanctions. This article argues that language equity in the GenAI era should be treated as a legality and academic ethics problem of assessment design. A defensible approach must separate legitimate language support (grammar, clarity, translation for comprehension, and surface-level editing) from impermissible substitution (generation or material restructuring of the reasoning, analysis, evidence, or sources the assessment intends to evaluate). Drawing on indirect discrimination logic, procedural fairness principles, and the academic integrity values of fairness and responsibility – and anchoring the analysis in an argument-based account of assessment validity – the article develops a model policy architecture and accompanying assessment design levers. The framework is purpose-based rather than tool-based, uses calibrated disclosure that is feasible for multilingual cohorts, and links enforcement to reasons, proportionality, and evidence standards that recognise the limits of detection-led reasoning. The contribution is a transferable governance blueprint for universities seeking to preserve standards while avoiding predictable, cohort-skewed unfairness in GenAI-era assessment.

## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.7321678`

- title: ANCORA Flow: A Conceptual Model for Change Management and AI Adoption with Psychological Safety, Sensemaking, and Sociotechnical Governance
- authors: Rosana Silvestre Torres
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-21
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7321678
- keyword hits: generative ai

### abstract

This white paper introduces the ANCORA Flow, a proprietary conceptual model for guiding artificial intelligence adoption and organizational change with explicit focus on combined maturity diagnosis, psychological safety, and sociotechnical governance. The model organizes transformation as a continuous six-phase cycle — Assess, Name, Cultivate, Orchestrate, Reinforce, and Anchor — built through integrative synthesis of established change management literature (Lewin, Bridges, Kotter, Hiatt), organizational maturity and readiness models (MIT CISR, MITRE, Gartner, Weiner, Blumberg and Pringle), the social neuroscience of behavior (Rock), psychological safety (Edmondson), organizational sensemaking (Weick), leadership storytelling (Denning), organizational culture (Schein; Cameron and Quinn), and AI governance frameworks (NIST, OECD, UNESCO, ISO/IEC 42001). The model's central contribution is repositioning psychological safety and combined organizational-individual diagnosis as structural preconditions for sustainable generative AI adoption, rather than desirable byproducts. Although developed in the context of generative AI adoption — the most urgent technological transformation currently underway — the ANCORA Flow's architecture derives largely from general change-management and human-behavior theory, not AI-specific literature, suggesting applicability to any technological or structural change process involving people. The model is presented as a conceptual contribution: it has not yet undergone systematic empirical validation, a limitation stated explicitly in this document as a future research agenda rather than a concealed gap.

## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.7319538`

- title: Managing Online Content Platforms with AI-Generated Content: Content Amplification and Disclosure Enforcement
- authors: Qiyuan Deng, Xin Fang, Linqiu Li
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-21
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7319538
- keyword hits: generative artificial intelligence

### abstract

Problem definition: Online content platforms face a growing governance challenge when creators use generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) without disclosure. We study how the platform's content amplification, which expands content reach, interacts with creators' manual effort, GenAI use, and disclosure choices. We compare two enforcement approaches: relabeling, which attaches a label of AI-generated content (AIGC) when nondisclosure is detected, and penalizing, which removes verified noncompliant content and truncates its remaining circulation and lifetime revenue. Methodology/results: We develop a game-theoretic model of a content platform and a representative creator. The platform decides whether to amplify the content, while the creator chooses manual effort, GenAI effort, and whether to disclose GenAI use. Undisclosed AIGC that escapes the platform's detection exposes the platform to external regulatory sanctions. We find that more effective amplification encourages hybrid human-AI production when AIGC aversion is mild but can induce a switch to manual production when aversion becomes stronger. Consequently, the platform's amplification incentive can be non-monotonic in AIGC aversion. In addition, better detection of AIGC can either help or hurt the platform, depending on regulatory pressure, and stricter external regulation may reduce both platform and creator profits by inducing the platform to withdraw amplification. Comparing the two enforcement approaches, we find penalizing can induce truthful disclosure and may improve outcomes for the platform, creator, and users simultaneously when AIGC aversion is mild and amplification e!ectiveness is intermediate, although neither approach dominates uniformly. Managerial implications: Platforms should jointly design content amplification, AIGC detection, and disclosure enforcement. The preferred enforcement approach depends on user attitudes toward AIGC, amplification effectiveness, and regulatory pressure. Regulators should also account for platforms' endogenous responses, because stricter regulation may suppress amplification without eliminating undisclosed AIGC and can thereby reduce both platform and creator payoffs.

## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.7320479`

- title: Design and Implementation of a Hybrid Economic Expert System for MSMEs in Nigeria
- authors: Gideon Ikebudeh
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-21
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7320479
- keyword hits: gemini

### abstract

The complexity of business environments in developing economies such as Nigeria has intensified demand for intelligent decisionsupport systems capable of interpreting dynamic economic conditions and assisting Micro, Small, and Medium Enterprises (MSMEs) in strategic planning. This study presents the design and implementation of a Hybrid Economic Expert System that integrates rule-based reasoning with machine learning analytics to support decision-making in taxation, compliance, funding, pricing, forecasting, and policy evaluation. The system was designed against three stated objectives: at least 85% accuracy in economic forecasting within a simulated environment, coverage of at least five distinct economic decision scenarios, and a user satisfaction rate of at least 80% in usability testing. Development followed a hybrid of Object-Oriented Analysis and Design Methodology (OOADM) and Agile methodology, decomposing the system into input, inference, output, and user interface components. The inference engine combines deterministic rule-based models for policy and tax computation with predictive machine learning models for trend forecasting. The system was implemented using Django, ReactJS, and SQLite, employing the Gemini and Hugging Face APIs for natural-language explanation of analytical outputs. A functional prototype covering nine advisory modules was completed and deployed for local testing. Scope of results reported. This report documents the design, architecture, and implementation of the prototype. The formal evaluation against the stated accuracy and satisfaction objectives has not yet been conducted; the test plan and instruments are specified in Chapter Four, and results will be reported on completion. No performance figures are claimed in advance of that evaluation.

## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.7322398`

- title: Improving Today, Narrowing Tomorrow: Collective Learning, Diversity, and Generativity
- authors: Esteve Almirall, Christopher Tucci
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-21
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7322398
- keyword hits: generative ai

### abstract

Generative AI makes a paradox newly visible: learning from a common source can improve what each firm does today while narrowing the variety available for tomorrow's discoveries. The tension extends beyond AI. Fields turn recurring features of successful organizations into portable practices-just-in-time production, freemium business models, or mixture-of-experts architectures. These collective abstractions are partial templates others can adapt, not designs or universal prescriptions. We study how they shape discovery in a model of firms searching interdependent landscapes. A collective repertoire extracts practices from patterns shared by leading configurations; firms vary in how they evaluate those practices locally. The model reveals a generativity chain: population diversity supplies heterogeneous experience, collective abstraction turns it into reusable options, and situated judgment matches options to local needs. Collective learning is not inherently homogenizing. Copying leaders consumes diversity quickly, whereas repertoires paired with local judgment preserve generative capacity. Environmental disruption exposes the consequences: it devalues accumulated knowledge but reopens local search, helping homogeneous, exhausted industries while harming diverse industries whose repertoire remains useful-even though diverse industries still perform better overall. Recurring practices may also outlive their usefulness, making popularity a poor test of validity after change. These findings matter for organizations using generative AI, best-practice communities, and research fields converging on dominant strands. Generativity requires governing the diversity feeding knowledge and the judgment applied to it. Learning systems should be judged not only by the answers they spread today, but by whether they preserve the differences from which tomorrow's answers can be made.

## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.7322038`

- title: The Two Practices of AI Consulting
- authors: Oshin Anand
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-21
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7322038
- keyword hits: generative ai

### abstract

Enterprise adoption of generative AI has produced an unusually wide gap between spending and measurable return. This paper argues that a substantial share of that gap originates earlier in the process than the prevailing explanations allow. Where the current literature locates failure in adoption, change management, workflow redesign or measurement, all of which are real and all of which are downstream, this paper locates it in the design of the solution itself. The argument rests on a distinction between two kinds of expert work. Until recently, an applied AI engagement was largely a construction project: preparing data, labelling it, training and tuning a model, monitoring the result, and repeating that sequence for each new problem. Pretrained models have absorbed most of that construction, and this paper accepts that displacement fully. What they have not absorbed is the architecture around it. A large model performs many kinds of tasks; a business problem is almost never one task. Deciding which capability addresses which component of a problem, where the boundaries between components fall, how each stage is evaluated, what happens when a component fails, and whether the arrangement survives the next model generation, remains expert work drawing on the same fundamentals the construction era demanded, applied one level up. The generative model is a component inside the design. It is not the design. From this distinction the paper derives a practical taxonomy of AI advisory work. Problem-first engagements begin with a defined business problem and require architecture. Tool-first engagements begin with a licence already purchased and require the recovery of an intent that was never stated. The two differ in their first question, their expertise and their deliverable, and most failed engagements are one practice sold at the other's price. Four principles for buying and selling AI advisory work follow, framed for executives who are currently commissioning vendors, evaluating renewals, or building internal capability. The contribution is conceptual rather than empirical. The argument is grounded in institutional evidence on enterprise AI outcomes, in the documented history of enterprise software implementation, and in the author's experience designing and delivering applied AI systems in production settings.
