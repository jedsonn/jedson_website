- authors: Sparsh Singh, Nitish Kumar, Amit Sharma
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2025-12-17
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.5918322
- keyword hits: large language model, large language models, llm, llms

### abstract

Modern mobile health (mHealth) applications are often encumbered by two critical limitations: a dependency on persistent internet connectivity and the use of static, non-adaptive algorithms for user guidance. To address these challenges, this paper presents VitaMate, a novel hybrid, offline-first AI framework for personalized fitness, implemented in React Native. Our performance evaluation validates the efficacy of this hybrid model; core on-device operations, including UI updates and natural language understanding, achieve a median latency of under 5 ms. In contrast, the non-critical, cloud-based AI task of generating a personalized seven-day workout plan completes in approximately 14.5 seconds. The primary contribution of this work is a practical and efficient architecture that resolves the inherent trade-off between the responsiveness and privacy of edge computing and the advanced personalization capabilities of cloud-based Large Language Models (LLMs) in the mHealth domain.

This paper presents an academic study on the application of AI in higher communication studies. The automotive industry is experiencing an unprecedented growth in software complexity driven by electrification, autonomy, and connectivity. Model-Based Design (MBD) using tools like Simulink has become standard in the industry, but the translation of natural language software requirements into compliant, tested control models remains a largely manual and time-consuming task. This paper presents a novel multi-agent large language model (LLM) framework tailored for automotive model-based software development. ## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.6740799`

- title: AI AS A THIRD-PARTY CONSULTANT? THE CORPORATE ATTORNEY-CLIENT PRIVILEGE & WORK PRODUCT DOCTRINE APPLIED TO GENERATIVE AI
- authors: Michele DeStefano
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-05-09
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.6740799
- keyword hits: chatgpt, claude, generative ai

### abstract

Lawyers routinely rely on generative AI platforms--both general-purpose tools such as Claude and ChatGPT and legally focused platforms such as Harvey, Legora, and CoCounsel--to analyze transactions, assess risk, draft documents, and structure legal advice for corporate clients. Currently, however, there is no agreement among federal courts on whether the attorney-client privilege or work product doctrine protects these communications. Four landmark 2026 decisions frame the debate. United States v. Heppner (S.D.N.Y. Feb. 17, 2026) held that documents a criminal defendant generated using a consumer AI platform were not privileged or protected as work product. Warner v. Gilbarco, Inc. (E.D. Mich. Feb. 10, 2026) reached the opposite conclusion, holding that AI-assisted litigation materials constitute protected work product and that treating AI tools as third parties for waiver purposes would "nullify work-product protection in nearly every modern drafting environment." Morgan v. V2X, Inc. (D. Colo. Mar. 30, 2026) substantially extended Warner, analogizing AI to email, phones, and smart devices, and introduced AI-specific protective order language. And the Sixth Circuit in United States v. Farris (Apr. 3, 2026) issued ethical guidance emphasizing safeguarding privilege when attorneys use AI. The critical unresolved question is how courts will treat attorney-directed use of closed, proprietary AI systems that contractually prohibit vendor access to client inputs. This Article makes three main arguments. First, the work product doctrine, although more easily applied to AI, is insufficient because it applies only in anticipation of litigation. Much of the most consequential AI-assisted legal work--M&A due diligence, regulatory compliance, contract drafting--occurs in transactional contexts where work product offers no protection. This limitation makes essential the privilege recommendations this Article proposes. Second, it argues that waiver analysis related to use of AI should be similar to that applied to other third-party vendors like e-discovery vendors, or contract analytics tools used by lawyers. As long as there is a reasonable expectation of confidentiality, Federal courts have never held that uploading privileged materials to contract analytics software, e-discovery platforms, or legal research tools constitutes a third-party disclosure that waives privilege or work product protection. Third, this Article argues that AI platforms should be analyzed under the same doctrine applied to human third-party consultants. AI platforms are functionally the most capable consultants in the history of the legal profession. The question is not whether they are "real" third parties in a philosophical sense, but whether they perform functions that, when performed by humans, courts have protected or declined to protect. However, applying that framework reveals that the emerging doctrine is at once potentially too narrow and too broad. Under the narrowest interpretation, use of AI would be categorically excluded from the privilege framework even when the substantive conditions for privilege—attorney direction, legal purpose, maintained confidentiality— are fully satisfied. Under the broadest application, courts might protect all inputs to, outputs from, and communications with a closed AI platform. This is because when AI is involved, the “legal purpose” inquiry becomes so easy to satisfy on the attorney's side and extremely hard to successfully challenge on the opponent's side, particularly in circuits applying the significant purpose test. This creates a de facto zone of secrecy even though the doctrine formally requires legal purpose, a problem amplified by the unresolved circuit split between the primary purpose and significant purpose tests left intact by the Supreme Court's dismissal of In re Grand Jury as improvidently granted. This Article proposes analysis under a single, uniform agency-based standard requiring a strong nexus between the AI's function and the legal advice ultimately provided, assessed through four factors: (1) who directed the AI use; (2) the nature of the platform--enterprise and closed, or consumer and open; (3) what is being sought to be protected--an output, input, prompt, or communication exchange; and (4) the purpose of the use, specifically whether providing legal advice was a primary or significant purpose. Unlike a categorical rule, this multi-factored nexus test embraces the essential role AI plays in the provision of high-quality legal advice while preventing corporations from using AI-laundered communications as a discovery shield.

## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.6732658`

- title: A Semantic Reinterpretation of the Language Generation Mechanism of Large Language Models - Focusing on the Dual Structure of Mathematical Language States and the Translation into Human Language -
- authors: Deokju Jegal
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-05-21
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.6732658
- keyword hits: large language model, large language models, llm, llms

### abstract

This study reinterprets the language generation process of large language models (LLMs) not as a direct production of human language, but as a process of translating a mathematically stabilized internal language state into human language. Whereas prior discussions have either explained artificial intelligence ’ s linguistic capability as a simulation of human language understanding or, conversely, reduced it to pure probabilistic computation, this study focuses on the dual linguistic strata inherent in the language generation process. Specifically, the vector operations and probability distribution formation performed within an LLM constitute an “ AI-specific linguistic state ” that is prior to human language, and only at the final token output stage does a form interpretable as human language emerge. To demonstrate this, the study analyzes the generation process step by step — from tokens and embedding space through the inference stages (attention and feed-forward operations) to the output stage — while juxtaposing engineering explanations with semantic interpretations at each stage. By arguing that meaning is not a fixed unit residing within language itself but a phenomenon formed in the transitional process between pre-linguistic computational states and linguistic interpretation, this study proposes the theoretical possibility of extending the scope of traditional semantics beyond human language to encompass language generation structures in general.

## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.6900538`

- title: Predicting the Pitch or Repeating the Public? Large Language Models and Sports Consensus Bias in the FIFA World Cup 2026
- authors: Nouar Aldahoul, Hezerul Abdul Karim, Myles Joshua Tan
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-06-23
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.6900538
- keyword hits: large language model, large language models, llm, llms

### abstract

As the FIFA World Cup 2026 approaches, global excitement is peaking and everyone is focused on predicting who will lift the trophy. However, football remains difficult to forecast because human emotion, unexpected injuries, and pure pitch drama constantly defy even the most advanced statistics. This work looks at whether different Large Language Models (LLMs) give different predictions or if they all just copy each other when simulating sports tournaments. Our study was done in three phases. In the first phase, we used 30 different LLMs to predict the winners of 72 individual matches in the group stage (ignoring draws scenarios). Surprisingly, there was low variety Because all 30 models selected the same winning team in at least 94% of the matches. In the second phase, we picked eight major LLMs from different companies and gave them a harder task. Instead of just picking a match winner, we asked them to simulate an entire four-team group stage consisting of six matches' total. We instructed them to look at team quality, world rankings, and tactics, and we forced them to include realistic draws. Even with this extra complexity in the prompt, all eight models still generated the same final group standings shown in the first phase. In the third phase, with the same eight LLMs, we instructed the prompt not to use reference, or factor in FIFA rankings, Elo ratings, or any numerical team coefficients. The prompt relies entirely on LLM's intrinsic knowledge. Even with this prompt, LLMs generated at least 70% of the same rankings and 90% of the same first and last place rankings shown in previous two phases. These results indicate that today's LLMs suffer from consensus bias when looking at public sports data. The models just repeat the most popular public opinions and standard world rankings found in their training data. Since the FIFA 2026 matches have not yet been played, it is not yet possible to evaluate how accurately the LLM predictions match the actual outcomes. This evaluation will be conducted once the tournament results become available.
