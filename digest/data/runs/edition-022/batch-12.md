- title: BanglaCompound-HW: A Handwritten Bangla Compound Character Image Dataset for OCR and Deep Learning Research
- authors: Srabonti Rozario, Abir Mahmud Shahariar, Sanjana Islam Kasfia, Md. Ismiel Hossen Abir
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-19
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7308559
- keyword hits: large language model, large language models, llm, llms

### abstract

Bangla is one of the most spoken languages in the world. However, handwritten Bangla character recognition is challenging because the script contains many compound characters. These compound characters often have similar shapes, complex structures, and different writing patterns. Therefore, it is challenging for Large Language Models (LLMs) to identify characters accurately. To solve the compound character-related issues, we develop the Bangla Compound Handwritten dataset. The dataset contains approximately 3,000 images from 30 commonly used Bangla compound character classes. Around 100 handwritten samples were collected for each class from 10 volunteer contributors with different handwriting styles. The dataset has a balanced class distribution and contains approximately 100 images for each class. It also contains natural variations in handwriting, which can help improve model generalization. Researchers can use this dataset for character classification, OCR benchmarking, image classification, and deep learning experiments. The dataset is publicly available through Mendeley Data.

## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.7290299`

- title: Unlearning The Machine: Bridging The Gap Between AI Architecture and the 'Right to Erasure' Under GDPR & DPDP Act, 2023
- authors: Vaseem Ali Khan
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-19
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7290299
- keyword hits: large language model, large language models, llm, llms

### abstract

Modern data privacy frameworks, such as Europe's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and India's Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Act, 2023, guarantee individuals the legal right to mandate the erasure of their personal data. However, these legal architectures are built upon the architectural assumptions of traditional relational databases where data deletion is binary and straightforward. Artificial Intelligence systems, particularly Large Language Models (LLMs), operate on a completely different paradigm. During training, personal data is absorbed, broken down, and permanently distributed across billions of non-linear mathematical "weights." This technical reality creates a "baking problem," where extracting an individual's data without discarding the entire model is functionally impossible. This paper analyzes the severe compliance crisis facing Data Fiduciaries and Controllers caught between absolute statutory requirements and technological constraints. Finally, it explores emerging tech-legal remedies, emphasizing the legal recognition of Machine Unlearning and the institutionalization of regulatory sandboxes to reconcile algorithmic longevity with individual privacy rights.

## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.7309138`

- title: Adjudicating Pro Se Litigation in the Era of Generative AI
- authors: Elissa Jacob
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-19
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7309138
- keyword hits: generative ai, large language model, llm, llms

### abstract

Appeals by judges, scholars, attorneys, and even litigants for reform to our overburdened court systems have largely gone unheard and unanswered for decades. Now that generative AI has become publicly accessible, the state of our courts is quickly becoming more of a crisis than an ongoing concern. Scholarship to date has largely focused on how AI is transforming the ways lawyers do work, and its potential to either help them be more efficient or its propensity to produce errant filings filled with “hallucinations.” However, AI’s impact on how unrepresented litigants navigate legal problems has garnered less attention, despite the skyrocketing rates of pro se filings since LLMs became publicly accessible, while the number of represented claims has remained stagnant. Because pro se claimants face much adversity and bias when seeking justice, submit the majority of all filings, and are more difficult to govern, I contend that how we regulate pro se AI usage is just as critical an inquiry as how we deal with attorney AI use. Currently, most courts do not have any AI-specific policies. The minority of courts that have chosen to address this issue are doing so through individual standing orders or court-specific local rules rather than by jurisdiction-wide rules. These orders vary greatly from prohibiting all AI use to merely warning litigants of their ethical duty to submit accurate representations to the court. Accordingly, this Article advocates for uniform, jurisdiction-wide standards that address AI pro se litigation. These standards must contend with the reality that the majority of pro se litigation is aided by large language model drafting (or soon will be), regardless of court rules, and clearly warn unrepresented claimants of the consequences of using hallucinated cases. Additionally, sanctioning rules and vexatious litigant laws should be expanded to more adequately address the unique and prevalent issue of pro se abuse of generative AI in ways that do not unfairly punish accidental AI missteps. Finally, courts, legal aid organizations, and law libraries should take supplemental steps to support generative AI as an access to justice tool by expanding access to legal research tools for checking the veracity of cases and updating legal aid educational materials to cover effective and ethical AI use.

## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.7298099`

- title: Scoping Review of Large Language Models in Medicine: Focus on Healthcare Provider Attitudes
- authors: Julija Vaitonyte
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-19
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7298099
- keyword hits: large language model, large language models, llm, llms

### abstract

This scoping review examined healthcare providers' use, attitudes, and the perceived benefits and ethical concerns regarding Large Language Models (LLMs), covering the period from 2023 to early 2026 following the widespread popularization of LLMs in late 2022. Relying on PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses), a literature search was performed in three databases, Google Scholar, PubMed, and Web of Science. The initial search identified 570 papers, of which 65 articles were assessed at full-text level by two reviewers. Of these, 25 met the inclusion criteria. They were analyzed descriptively to provide a synthesis of the available literature. The included articles revealed an interest in the LLM technology among healthcare providers. While the overall awareness of LLMs was high, it was only partially reflected in their usage. Between 18% and 56% of clinicians reported having used an LLM for clinical practice, with the use being infrequent. Nurses tended to use LLMs for learning, with between 20% and 57% indicating any use. The LLM technology was mostly viewed as beneficial for documentation and administrative tasks, patient education, as well as searching and summarizing medical information. The primary ethical concerns raised included the accuracy of information and data privacy. In sum, healthcare providers' attitudes toward LLMs reflected a mixture of excitement and concerns, however LLMs were not conceived of as being ready for the incorporation in clinical practice.

## uid: `arxiv:2608.18613v1`

- title: CTIFoundry: An Agent-Native Corpus Scaffold for Cyber Threat Intelligence
- authors: Yutong Cheng, Changze Li, Qian Cui, Wei Ding, Lingzhi Wang, Yan Chen, Peng Gao
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-19
- source: arXiv
- link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18613v1
- keyword hits: agentic, claude, llm, retrieval-augmented

### abstract

Cyber threat intelligence (CTI) is increasingly consumed not by human analysts but by LLM agents that compose multi-step investigations at query time. The harness side of this shift has matured rapidly (planning loops, tool protocols, context management), but the corpus side has not: threat reports and vulnerability databases are still packaged for retrieval-augmented generation, as opaque chunks behind an embedding index. We argue that this substrate, not model capability, is the bottleneck on agentic CTI investigation, and present CTIFoundry, an agent-native corpus scaffold. At build time, CTIFoundry materializes the latent structure of a CTI corpus: a deterministic ontology graph over four authoritative knowledge bases (CVE, CWE, CAPEC, ATT&CK) whose official cross-references become typed, traversable edges; a span-grounded report layer whose canonical, alias-resolved cross-vendor entities index provenance-carrying chunks; and hybrid dense+lexical retrieval surfaces. At query time this structure is exposed through seven typed tools and three procedural skills mounted on a stock open-source agent harness. On the public CTIConnect benchmark, swapping only the action surface lifts the identically-harnessed agent by +0.19 to +0.28 overall F1 across a four-model, two-provider panel: a small model on CTIFoundry surpasses a flagship on the flat substrate, and the gain is not bought with search effort, since on both Claude models the scaffolded agent is more accurate at roughly half the tool calls. An ablation attributes it: typed structure carries the larger share, procedural skills convert structure into discipline, and the two compose super-additively, because skills bind only to structure that exists.

## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.7320247`

- title: When Machines Read Between the Lines Differently: AI Disagreement in Financial Analysis
- authors: Ryan Pelkey, Michael A. Quinn
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-20
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7320247
- keyword hits: chatgpt, claude, gemini

### abstract

Artificial intelligence tools are revolutionizing how business professionals use and interpret the information around them. The financial industry has moved even farther with the creation of fully automated AI-powered investment advisors. However, there exists a shortage of research into the reliability and consistency of these tools. This paper presents evidence regarding the consistency of different AI tools interpreting annual reports of Standard & Poor’s 100 companies. ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude produce significant differences in how they score predictions of firms’ future profitability. Known as sentiment scores, these values differed both in levels and in relative company rankings. ChatGPT and Gemini had less variation in values than Claude. The AI tools showed differences in how they scored large versus small firms as well, suggesting that firm size could be a factor biasing results. Overall, these results imply that professionals in the financial industry should use caution in how they implement AI tools to assist in interpretations and predictions. The resulting recommendations of whether to buy and sell, determination of credit risk, and valuations may vary based on which AI tool is used. A financial market in which professionals are using different AI tools could contribute to differences in valuations and investment decisions unrelated to fundamentals.

## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.7306183`

- title: Do Richer Personas Improve LLM Survey Simulation? A Fidelity Paradox
- authors: Andrew Gordon, Nora Petrova, John Burden, Oriol Bosch, Ning Ding
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-20
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7306183
- keyword hits: large language model, large language models, llm, llms

### abstract

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly proposed as substitutes for human survey respondents, despite documented failures in their accuracy and realism. We field a 21-item survey on political opinion and consumer insights to a politically representative online US sample (N = 996), supplement it with AI-moderated qualitative interviews, and attempt to reproduce the human data with LLM-simulated respondents at increasing levels of persona fidelity, from no information at all, through demographic personas, to demographics plus verbatim idiographic interview transcripts. We find a fidelity paradox in that richer persona context does not straightforwardly improve accuracy. Individual-level simulation with demographic personas, a dominant "silicon sampling" design, roughly triples distributional error relative to simply asking the model for an aggregate distribution, due to severe variance collapse. We show this collapse is largely a reporting artefact rather than a knowledge deficit. Eliciting a per-persona probability distribution instead of a single forced answer improves distributional accuracy by 35 to 48 percent while preserving the individual-level data subgroup analysis requires. Post-stratifying the best synthetic datasets through the same MRP model used for the human data leaves distributional error unchanged, marking the residual as model error that survey correction cannot reach, and even the best pipeline never approaches the sampling-noise floor two real polls of this size would share. Throughout, distributional fidelity and point accuracy systematically dissociate, so no single statistic certifies synthetic data as accurate, only accurate for a specified target. These conclusions replicate across two frontier models, and support using LLM simulation for directional, exploratory work rather than as a substitute for measurement.

## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.7318518`

- title: Operationalizing Longitudinal Machine Information Behavior: The Founding and Developmental Pilot of MIBO: An Open Resource and Methods Paper
- authors: Kento Sasano
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-20
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7318518
- keyword hits: chatgpt, claude, gemini

### abstract

Public generative-AI services are non-stationary information intermediaries, but longitudinal study is difficult when model, retrieval, policy, access, and source conditions change together. This resource and methods paper documents the founding and developmental Pilot of the Machine Information Behavioral Observatory (MIBO), launched in Japan on 5 May 2026. Building on prior work on autonomous and machine information behavior, machine behaviour, longitudinal evaluation, and external auditing, the paper contributes an operational implementation rather than a terminology or priority claim. The Pilot conducted weekly API-based observation waves across ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and Perplexity. The evidence release freezes Day 1–Day 13 and contains 244 included service-query observations, using a version-controlled bilingual instrument that expanded from three to five fixed queries; later Pilot observations are outside this release. We specify the observation unit, phase boundary, repository architecture, coding layers, source normalization, correction ledger, and claim lifecycle. Four design-validation cases show why citation form, source use, entity ontology, advice categories, parameters, and data corrections must be recorded separately. The Pilot also exposed incomplete preservation of some early API parameters, absence of within-cell replication, and single-investigator coding. These limitations directly informed a separate, preregistration-ready MIBO Core protocol. The principal contribution is a corrigible public infrastructure for maintaining inspectable service-level information-behavior records over calendar time.
