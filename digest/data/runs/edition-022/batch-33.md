## uid: `arxiv:2608.18758v1`

- title: Epistemic Subordination: Generative AI and the Infrastructure of Knowledge
- authors: Gilad Abiri, Emanuel V. Towfigh
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-19
- source: arXiv
- link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18758v1
- keyword hits: generative ai

### abstract

Generative AI does not merely produce biased outputs. It encodes the majority's way of knowing as the default infrastructure of knowledge itself. We call this epistemic subordination. The training process compresses the full breadth of human expression into a single probabilistic model whose statistical baseline reflects the languages, assumptions, and cultural frameworks of the dominant culture. Minority epistemologies are not excluded but absorbed: present in the training data, yet structurally subordinated in the output. The result is not a collection of discrete biases that can be audited and corrected. It is an epistemic condition embedded in the architecture from which all outputs emerge. This unified harm cuts across three legal domains -- anti-discrimination law, cultural and linguistic rights, and democratic viewpoint pluralism -- and each fails to address it for the same structural reason: existing law regulates downstream, at the level of decisions and applications. The remedy must match the site of harm. If epistemic subordination is produced at the level of model training, then law must learn to govern at that level.

## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.7314483`

- title: Can AI-assisted Awards Survive the New York Convention? A Doctrinal Assessment of Enforcement Risk in the Age of the Algorithmic Arbitrator
- authors: Fernanda Pires Merouco
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-20
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7314483
- keyword hits: generative ai

### abstract

This article examines whether the New York Convention’s existing framework for refusing recognition and enforcement of arbitral awards is equipped to address the rise of AI- assisted arbitration. Drawing on recent institutional initiatives — including the AAA-ICDR’s AI Arbitrator, private AI mediation platforms, and the UNCITRAL Colloquium on Artificial Intelligence in Dispute Resolution — as well as emerging case law such as LaPaglia v. Valve Corp. and ARIHQ v. Santé Québec, the article tests six distinct challenges raised by generative AI against the Convention’s text. It asks, in turn, whether an award presupposes a human decision-maker; whether AI drafting reviewed only nominally by a human arbitrator amounts to an impermissible delegation of authority; whether AI-generated reasoning can satisfy reasoned-award requirements; whether embedded algorithmic bias escapes the Convention’s disclosure-and-challenge apparatus; whether party consent can neutralize these risks; and whether the Convention remains an adequate living instrument or requires targeted amendment. The article concludes that the Convention’s existing grounds for refusal, tested against each of these challenges, adequately address them without requiring amendment. A treaty designed to test the soundness of human judgment, the article argues, has no difficulty identifying where that judgment is missing.

## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.7318319`

- title: Cognitive Effort in Large Language Models: A New Mirror for Seeing Human Reasoning
- authors: Ramin Saadat
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-20
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7318319
- keyword hits: large language model, large language models

### abstract

A recent capability released in one large language model lets the user set the amount of computational "effort" the model spends, independently of which model is chosen. For the first time, this feature separates two axes of reasoning that have always been intertwined in humans, presenting them as distinct parameters: the ceiling of capability, and the quantity of resources spent on a given problem. This paper uses that technical separation as an analytical tool for seeing the mechanism of human reasoning more clearly. We first describe machine reasoning on the basis of evidence and propose a three-dimensional framework: capability, effort, and calibration (the degree to which confidence matches correctness). We then adopt an explicit methodological position, namely that the difference between machine and human traces back to their material substrate while the structure of their reasoning is assumed, within reason, to be alike, and we look for these three dimensions in humans. We argue that all three are identifiable in humans, but with one difference: what is independent and neutral in the machine is, in humans, entangled with motivation and identity. The focus of this entanglement lies in the dimension of effort, which we call "the lever problem". In the machine, an external and impartial agent sets the lever of effort, whereas in humans an interested mind holds it. From this angle we propose a redefinition of motivated reasoning: not a flaw in the quality of logical steps, but an asymmetric allocation of effort in favour of the desired conclusion. On the historical side, we argue that the history of wisdom can be read, in part, as a history of the unequal distribution of the opportunity for effort, and that the machine makes this scarce resource universal, not by adding to capability, but by changing the form and efficiency of intellectual work. The paper claims no proof; it offers an analytical framework and several open questions. Perhaps the value of the machine lies not in its resemblance to us, but in that very difference: a mirror of another kind, in which we see, in a decomposed state, what in us was always unified and hidden.

## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.7292289`

- title: Ethical Integration of Generative Artificial Intelligence in Healthcare Education: A Discussion on Opportunities, Challenges, and Future Directions
- authors: Kashif Khan
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-20
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7292289
- keyword hits: generative artificial intelligence

### abstract

Generic computational intelligence (Genial) is swiftly revolutionizing healthcare education by transforming instruction, evaluation, and the acquisition of clinical knowledge. The increasing use presents opportunities for individualized education, enhanced educational efficacy, and evidence-based decision-making; however, it also raises substantial concerns about academic integrity, analytical thinking, and professional excellence. This discussion paper rigorously analyses the benefits and challenges of incorporating Genial into healthcare education, with a focus on ethical application and workforce readiness. The analysis indicates that overreliance on AI-generated content may compromise students' independent reasoning, problem-solving skills, and long-term knowledge retention, thereby affecting future academic and clinical success, as well as patient safety. Moreover, inadequate AI literacy among students and instructors heightens the likelihood of improper AI use, disinformation, and compromised professional standards. To optimize the benefits of Genial and mitigate its drawbacks, healthcare organizations should implement comprehensive AI literacy initiatives, establish ethical governance structures, and reform assessment methodologies to evaluate advanced cognitive and clinical reasoning abilities rather than mere information retention. The judicious incorporation of Genial into healthcare education necessitates a harmonious blend of technological advancement, human discernment, ethical consciousness, and analytical reasoning. This method will enhance educational quality, uphold academic integrity, and cultivate a proficient healthcare workforce equipped to provide safe, ethical, and patient-centered care in an increasingly AI-driven healthcare landscape.

## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.7318100`

- title: From Prompt Consumers to Pedagogical Auditors: How Green Constraints and AI Integration Foster a Design Leap in Pre-Service Geography Teacher Education
- authors: Hossein Talebzadeh
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-20
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7318100
- keyword hits: generative ai

### abstract

The integration of generative AI into teacher education presents risks of cognitive offloading and passive consumption of automated outputs. This study examines a structured intervention combining Sustainable Development Pedagogy (zero-paper constraints) and the Integrated AI Triad framework. Conducted with 28 instructional design teams (N = 91 pre-service teachers) in a geography methodology course in Iran, the study followed three phases: baseline unconstrained AI use (T₁), internal zero-paper intervention (T₂), and public dissemination in an open repository (T₃). Repeated measures analysis revealed a substantial effect on design quality, F(2, 54) = 284.15, p < .001, ηp²= .913, with a significant secondary gain from T₂ to T₃ (Δ = +0.69, p < .001). Qualitative analysis indicated that local material constraints combined with global accountability mechanisms prompted a shift from passive reliance to strategic auditing of AI outputs. These findings suggest that, when systematically scaffolded, AI integration can enhance rather than erode teacher agency. The study offers a practical and replicable model for integrating AI into pre-service teacher education while sustaining pedagogical integrity and environmental responsibility, contributing to ongoing debates on AI literacy in higher education.

## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.7319939`

- title: From the governable person to the governing machine: Accounting research on government and digital artificial intelligence
- authors: Thomas Ahrens
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-20
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7319939
- keyword hits: large language model

### abstract

Accounting research has spent decades examining how states and corporations came to govern through calculative practices, and how the objectives of these artificial agents came to displace the interests of the humans they were created to serve. This paper argues that accounting research on government (ARG), developed to study states and corporations—or ”analogue artificial intelligence” (AAI) as David Runciman recently called them—can be repurposed to assess the promises now advanced by promoters of digital artificial intelligence (DAI). Read through ARG, DAI's promises of abundance, augmentation, and benefit to all of humanity are neither truths to be credited nor lies to be exposed, but programmatic justifications that assemble new apparatuses of government. The paper traces AAI's familiar government through territorialising, mediating, adjudicating, and subjectivising to contemporary DAI, and shows that the training of a large language model is itself a calculative apparatus constituting a governable subject in a very literal sense. It then identifies a key difference. Government by AAI produced governable human subjects and worked always through the people assembled into it. Government by DAI promises an agent that will occupy the evaluative standpoint itself and judge on humanity's behalf what is to count as humanity's benefit. The paper argues that the resulting hollowness of DAI's claim is not a consequence of the falsity of its promised goods but of its presentation of a contingent form of government as a necessary one. Exposing that claim without external reference to the human good, the paper retains a Foucauldian critique.

## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.7317713`

- title: Governing the Scam Ecosystem: Policy Responses to Generative AI-Enabled Deepfake Financial Fraud
- authors: Alice E. Marwick, Schiffrin Anya, Kaylee Williams
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-20
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7317713
- keyword hits: generative ai

### abstract

AI-enabled scams represent one of the most consequential and underregulated harms of the generative AI era. This paper examines how generative AI, and deepfakes in particular, have transformed fraud into a globalized, industrialized threat, and evaluates the adequacy of existing regulatory responses across platforms, telecommunications, and financial systems. Drawing on a comparative analysis of policy frameworks from more than a dozen jurisdictions, we find that current approaches are fragmented, reactive, and structurally misaligned with the transnational, multi-actor nature of the scam pipeline. Individual-responsibility models have consistently failed; shared liability frameworks, mandatory data-sharing requirements, and cross-border enforcement remain underdeveloped. We argue that effective governance requires a shift toward institutional accountability, anchored by the principle of the ”cheapest cost avoider”: social platforms, as the primary infrastructure through which scammers reach victims at scale, are best positioned to prevent harm and should bear commensurate liability when they do not. Where platforms fail to act, financial institutions represent the next critical intervention point. AI-enabled scams are not merely a consumer protection problem but a stress test of AI governance architecture, one that current frameworks are failing.
