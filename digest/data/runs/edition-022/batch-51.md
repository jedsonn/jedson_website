## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.4667572`

- title: AI Technologies in the Judiciary: Critical Appraisal of Large Language Models in Judicial Decision-making
- authors: Juan David Gutiérrez Rodríguez
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2024-01-10
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.4667572
- keyword hits: large language model, large language models, llm, llms

### abstract

Using large language models (LLMs) in judicial decision-making carries social, organizational, and individual risks. This chapter provides an account of how the judiciary around the World, but especially in Latin America, have used LLMs to draft rulings or take decisions during court hearings and to problematize such uses by judges and their clerks and how they have justified these applications. The chapter argues that current LLMs cannot be regarded as trustworthy sources of information even if their capacity to produce language outputs mimics human ones in often seemingly convincing ways. Hence, LLMs should only be used – with the utmost care – when other more effective and safe judicial decision-making options are unavailable. Moreover, the chapter contends that the judiciary should promote digital literacy and informed, transparent, ethical, and responsible use of artificial intelligence tools to reap their potential benefits and prevent risks.

## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.4718347`

- title: Asking GPT for the Ordinary Meaning of Statutory Terms
- authors: Christoph Engel, Richard H. McAdams
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2024-02-06
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.4718347
- keyword hits: chatgpt, large language model, llm, llms

### abstract

We report on our test of the Large Language Model (LLM) ChatGPT (GPT) as a tool for generating evidence of the ordinary meaning of statutory terms. We explain why the most useful evidence for interpretation involves a distribution of replies rather than only what GPT regards as the single “best” reply. That motivates our decision to use Chat 3.5 Turbo instead of Chat 4 and to run each prompt we use 100 times. Asking GPT whether the stat-utory term “vehicle” includes a list of candidate objects (e.g., bus, bicycle, skateboard) al-lows us to test it against a benchmark, the results of a high-quality experimental survey (Tobia 2000) that asked over 2,800 English speakers the same questions. After learning what prompts fail and which one works best (a belief prompt combined with a Likert scale reply), we use the successful prompt to test the effects of “informing” GPT that the term appears in a particular rule (one of five possible) or that the legal rule using the term has a particular purpose (one of six possible). Finally, we explore GPT’s sensitivity to meaning at a particular moment in the past (the 1950s) and its ability to distinguish extensional from intensional meaning. To our knowledge, these are the first tests of GPT as a tool for gen-erating empirical data on the ordinary meaning of statutory terms. Legal actors have good reason to be cautious, but LLMs have the potential to radically facilitate and improve legal tasks, including the interpretation of statutes.

- title: Evaluating the Efficacy of Generative Artificial Intelligence in Grading: Insights from Authentic Assessments in Economics
- authors: Minh  Nhat Nguyen, Binh Nguyen Thanh, Diem  Thi Hong Vo, Tra Pham Thi Thu, Hieu Thai, Son Ha Xuan
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2024-04-06
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.4752229
- keyword hits: chatgpt, generative ai, generative artificial intelligence

### abstract

This study explores how Generative Artificial Intelligence (AI) can assist educators in grading, especially in authentic assessments within economics education. Authentic assessments, crucial for developing 21st-century skills, often involve complex, take-home assignments that challenge students to apply theoretical knowledge to practical situations. These assignments, though essential, pose grading challenges for educators due to time constraints and potential biases. Addressing this, the study introduces a framework to assess Generative AI's effectiveness in grading, using tools like ChatGPT4. The framework involves comparing human expert evaluations with those generated by AI, across various cognitive levels of Bloom's taxonomy. The research specifically examines Generative AI's ability to grade text and data analysis questions, and its consistency with human markers, while also considering potential biases like halo and confirmation effects. The findings indicate that while ChatGPT4 aligns closely with human evaluations in lower-level cognitive tasks and text-based questions, it falls short in higher-level data analysis tasks. The study not only highlights the potential of Generative AI in enhancing grading efficiency and reducing biases in higher education but also points out its limitations, offering insights for educators and policymakers.

### abstract

Large Language Models (LLMs) have brought about a great leap forward in natural language processing, but the increasing demand for manipulation of multimodal data (multimodal: text and images, or audio, video) requires endowing LLMs with more enhanced capabilities to handle diverse kinds of processed input data efficiently. In this paper, we discuss the optimization strategies for LLMs with ability of handling multimodal data processing and integration at scale by taking care of scalability and computational overhead. ## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.5250994`

- title: Concept-Based AI (LLMs) vs. Token-Based Language Models (LCMs): The Philosophical Divide
- authors: Anthony Chidi Nzomiwu, Francisca Uzooyibo Okoye, Scholastica Uzondu
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2025-05-14
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.5250994
- keyword hits: large language model, large language models, llm, llms

### abstract

This article examines the philosophical and cognitive implications of the fundamental differences between token-based and concept-based approaches in artificial intelligence language models. While token-based models have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in linguistic pattern recognition and text generation, they fundamentally differ from human cognitive processing, which operates through conceptual understanding and embodied experience. This research synthesizes perspectives from philosophy of mind, cognitive science, and artificial intelligence to analyze the theoretical limitations of current language models and explores the potential pathways toward more concept-oriented AI systems. The symbol grounding problem emerges as a central philosophical challenge, highlighting how computational systems struggle to connect symbolic representations to meaningful understanding without embodied experience. Through critical examination of recent developments in large language models and their comparison to human cognition, this paper contributes to the ongoing discourse on machine understanding, consciousness, and the nature of artificial intelligence. The findings suggest that bridging the gap between token-based processing and concept-based understanding may require fundamental reconsiderations of how we approach artificial intelligence development.

## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.5400466`

- title: Selective Facts Can Be As Persuasive As Falsehoods
- authors: Jennifer Allen, Amir Tohidi, Samar Haider, David Rothschild, Duncan Watts
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2025-09-03
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.5400466
- keyword hits: large language model, large language models, llm, llms

### abstract

Concerns about fake news are widespread in part because falsehoods are seen as an effective way to mislead and inflame. However, other studies have shown that factually accurate but biased information can also affect beliefs and attitudes. Do falsehoods influence opinions more than selectively framed, but factual information? We address this question by conducting a randomized experiment that uses large language models (LLMs) to synthetically generate articles that systematically vary in frame (positive vs. negative) and veracity (selective facts vs. exaggerations vs. fabrications). We find that although lying has a large effect on downstream attitudes, selective presentation of factual statements from mainstream media has an effect that is statistically indistinguishable in magnitude. We extend this finding in two follow-up experiments that compare the persuasiveness of high-profile, documented falsehoods and selective facts on salient topics like the economy, immigration, and election integrity. In both of these follow ups, we find that both falsehoods and selective facts significantly influence opinion and no evidence that lies are consistently more persuasive. Notably, Study 2B, which focuses on the California Primary Election, finds that selective facts are significantly \textit{more} persuasive than outright falsehoods at decreasing trust in the election and increasing misbelief in widespread election fraud. Our findings challenge the notion that falsehoods are the only, or even the primary, concern with regard to the shaping of public misperceptions.
