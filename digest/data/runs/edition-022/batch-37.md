- title: AI-Mediated Knowledge Markets as Governable Digital Economies: A Structural Architecture for Provenance, Access, Settlement, and Systemic Trust
- authors: Y. Hori
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-21
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7324599
- keyword hits: generative ai

### abstract

AI assistants and generative AI systems are shifting knowledge creation, retention, and use from human-facing knowledge management processes toward automated, agent-mediated digital economies. As knowledge is discovered, evaluated, summarized, acquired, retained, recombined, and reused across platforms by autonomous agents, circulation can be amplified faster than provenance verification, access control, usage attribution, settlement, and trust mechanisms can update. This working paper conceptualizes AI-mediated knowledge markets as governable digital economies and proposes a conceptual and design-oriented structural architecture that couples provenance, access, settlement, and systemic trust. The central unit is the validated knowledge usage event: a bounded unit of access or reuse that is observable, admissible, and settleable. The paper does not propose a specific blockchain protocol, pricing algorithm, or cryptographic primitive; it offers a protocol-facing governance architecture for interoperable knowledge exchange. It demonstrates the design logic through a worked scenario and outlines diagnostics such as selection latency, provenance coverage, audit completeness, dispute rate, trust degradation, and post-policy leakage for assessing whether knowledge circulation remains governable under reinforcement. The contribution is architectural and conceptual; it does not empirically evaluate a deployed market, and it is intended to support later scenario-based analysis, replay simulation, adversarial stress testing, and case comparison.

## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.7323818`

- title: Understanding Students' Non-Use of ChatGPT: Attitudes, Concerns, and Recommendations
- authors: Shiva Hajian, Komal Uppal
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-21
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7323818
- keyword hits: chatgpt, generative artificial intelligence

### abstract

As generative artificial intelligence (AI) tools such as ChatGPT become increasingly integrated into higher education, research has largely focused on students who actively use these technologies, while the perspectives of non-users remain underexplored. Using descriptive frequency analysis of survey responses, this study examines the attitudes, perceptions, and concerns of 37 university students who do not use ChatGPT for academic purposes. The findings are organized into four areas: reasons for non-use, concerns about AI use, factors that may encourage future adoption, and overall perceptions of ChatGPT. The most common reasons for non-use were limited familiarity with ChatGPT and low perceived usefulness. The strongest factor that could encourage future adoption was the availability of clear institutional guidelines outlining appropriate AI use. Overall, students' perceptions were cautious but not strongly negative. These findings suggest that non-use may be influenced more by uncertainty, limited guidance, and lack of experience than by outright rejection of AI technologies. Higher education institutions can support responsible AI engagement by implementing structured AI literacy programs, providing practical training, and communicating clear and transparent policies. Future research should employ experimental and longitudinal approaches to examine how AI literacy and policy clarity influence AI adoption, accuracy, and ethical decision-making when students use tools such as ChatGPT.

## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.7323459`

- title: When the Vendor Changes the Firm: Vendor-Induced Routine Drift from Foundation Model Updates
- authors: Purabkumar Upadhyay
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-21
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7323459
- keyword hits: foundation model

### abstract

Organizations increasingly embed externally supplied foundation models into recurring managerial decision processes. Yet foundation-model providers can update underlying models while the adopting organization leaves its prompts, policies, workflows, decision criteria, employees, and business inputs unchanged. This paper introduces Vendor-Induced Routine Drift (VIRD) , defined as a systematic change in recurring organizational decision enactments attributable to a change in an externally controlled AI model while relevant organization-controlled conditions remain materially unchanged. Drawing on organizational-routine theory, human–AI decision structures, resource dependence, and research on foundation-model behavioral instability, the paper develops a theoretical framework explaining how vendor-side model updates may alter an organization's effective decision policy without a conventional internally initiated change process. The paper distinguishes VIRD from ordinary model stochasticity, data drift, concept drift, deliberate digital transformation, model switching, and endogenous routine adaptation. Six propositions are developed concerning model-version effects, decision ambiguity, AI autonomy, human review, policy codification, and organizational scale. A preregisterable empirical design is proposed using standardized managerial decision scenarios and repeated within-version measurements to distinguish systematic version-induced drift from stochastic variation. The framework reframes foundation-model upgrades as potential organizational change events rather than merely technical maintenance events.

### abstract

Digital manipulation poses severe risks to democratic governance, markets, and institutional legitimacy. This is the first article to conceptualize Trust Tech (TTech) as a distinct sector of profit-oriented firms developing technologies to detect, mitigate, or prevent digital manipulation and disinformation. Using an original hand-collected dataset of 256 companies worldwide, we provide the first sector-level empirical mapping of TTech and compare its development with adjacent sectors: influencer marketing and cybersecurity. ## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.7330465`

- title: What 5,000 Questions to AI Reveal About Children’s Curiosity and Information-Seeking Behavior
- authors: Sunhyo Oh, Lauren Girouard-Hallam, Ying Xu
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-22
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7330465
- keyword hits: ai agent, generative ai, prompting

### abstract

Generative AI is becoming an increasingly common source of information for children, creating new opportunities and challenges for how they formulate, evaluate, and follow up on information. In this study, we examined children’s information-seeking behaviors using a child-friendly, voice-driven AI system through a two-week home-based deployment, involving 52 children aged 8 to 10. Analyses of 5,158 question-answer exchanges between children and AI revealed that children primarily used AI not only to request factual information about general knowledge, about themselves, and about the AI agent but also to seek opinions and preferences from AI. Although children generally endorsed information provided by AI, their evaluations were sensitive to its quality (i.e., children were less likely to endorse responses that were inaccurate or irrelevant). Children’s individual differences also played a role in their evaluation patterns. Children with greater experience with AI were less likely to endorse AI responses, whereas children with weaker inhibitory control and more positive attitudes toward AI were more likely to do so. While children’s evaluations did not predict their subsequent information-seeking behavior, exploratory analyses indicated that the quality of AI responses shaped children’s subsequent information-seeking decisions, with insufficient responses more often prompting children to restate prior questions. The findings suggest that generative AI functions not only as a source of information but also as a conversational partner that shapes how children’s questions unfold over time, providing implications for developmental theory, design of child-facing AI systems, and educational practices.

## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.7274000`

- title: Multi-Stage Neural Networks with Learnable Intermediate Bridges: From a Theoretical Framework to Production Retrieval-Augmented Generation
- authors: Marcus Bergo
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-15
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7274000
- keyword hits: retrieval-augmented

### abstract

In 2018 we formalized a framework for compositional systems of three neural networks connected by learnable intermediate bridges-trainable interface modules governed by explicit contracts on shape, semantics, distributional stability, information content, and computational budget (Bergo, 2018). The central hypothesis was that structured decomposition with explicit interface contracts can outperform monolithic scaling whenever a task naturally separates into perception, inference, and generation stages. This article revisits that framework in light of a 2025 production deployment: a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) system in which a budget-constrained reranking "sidecar" realizes the perception-inference bridge and a QLoRA adapter (Dettmers et al., 2023) realizes the inference-generation bridge. In internal production evaluation, the deployed system exhibited no detected hallucinations on its evaluation suite, sustained a sidecar latency below 0.3 ms, and reduced generator input tokens by more than 90% relative to conventional RAG. We present the mathematical foundation, a taxonomy of bridge modules, the training methodology, an event-driven execution model, and an information-theoretic analysis-grounded in the information bottleneck principle (Tishby et al., 2000)-of why bridge-mediated context compression structurally suppresses hallucination. We argue that the interface between models, rather than the models themselves, is the primary design object for reliable multi-stage systems.
