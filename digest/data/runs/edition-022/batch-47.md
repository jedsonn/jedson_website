- title: Regulating automated decision-making in the Justice system: what is the problem?
- authors: David Mark, Tomás McInerney, John Morison
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2025-01-13
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.5021951
- keyword hits: chatgpt, large language model, large language models, llm, llms

### abstract

The integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) and judicial decision-making promises enhancements in efficiency and cost reduction, but simultaneously introduces substantial technical uncertainties and regulatory dilemmas. This situation is further complicated by the absence of specific legislation, and the pace at which new technologies, including ChatGPT and other Large Language Models (LLMs), are seizing hold. Within this lacuna we can identify diverging ‘problematisations’ of concerns by critical stakeholders. Government strategies often emphasise the economic potential of AI; judicial and legal associations tend to prioritise ethical and legitimacy considerations; and non-governmental organizations frequently focus on threats to individualised justice . Nonetheless, arising from this dialectic there is agreement on the need for transparency, accountability, and the indispensable role of human involvement in AI-judicial functions. This paper scrutinises the evolving regulatory context and key stakeholder positions, concluding that whilst AI may enhance the judicial role, existing proposals to mitigate seminal concerns could prove inadequate.

- authors: Martina Bajcic
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2025-01-27
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.5040587
- keyword hits: generative ai, large language model, large language models, llm, llms

### abstract

While there is no denying that new AI technologies and tools are making a significant impact on translation, specialized translation remains problematic for automation especially in regard to terminology. Precise and consistent terminology use requires particular attention in the field of legal translation as a sensitive area affecting the whole of the society. Most recently, tools based on large language models have been gaining attention as potential game changers in the area of legal translation. How do such tools cope with the complexity of legal terminology and legal texts? Can they be trusted to perform the task of legal translation? To investigate this, a two-stage research methodology is applied: first, an analysis of papers published on the topic of large language models and legal translation by relying on quantitative bibliographic and bibliometric indicators, and second, a qualitative content analysis of the retrieved papers. The results show that despite the unparalleled interest into the application of generative AI in all spheres of life, to date there has been scarce research on its application in the field of legal translation. The results of this study therefore provide detailed insight into the state-of-the-art research on this novel topic, tracing the current and proposing future research trajectories. With a view of examining the potential of using LLMs in legal translation, it is instrumental to conduct further empirical studies from interdisciplinary perspectives including diverse legal texts and both low and high-resourced languages.

### abstract

Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) have recently gained immense popularity. Powerful commercial models like ChatGPT-4V and Gemini, as well as open-source ones such as LLaVA, are essentially general-purpose models and are applied to solve a wide variety of tasks, including those in computer vision. These neural networks possess such strong general knowledge and reasoning abilities that they have proven capable of working even on tasks for which they were not specifically trained. ## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.5152523`

- title: ChatGPT, Esq.: Recasting Unauthorized Practice of Law in the Era of Generative AI
- authors: Joseph Avery, Patricia Abril, Alissa Del Riego
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2025-04-23
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.5152523
- keyword hits: chatgpt, generative ai, gpt-4, large language model, large language models

### abstract

In March of 2023, OpenAI released GPT-4, an autoregressive language model that uses deep learning to produce text. GPT-4 has unprecedented ability to practice law: drafting briefs and memos, plotting litigation strategy, and providing general legal advice. However, scholars and practitioners have yet to unpack the implications of large language models, such as GPT-4, for long-standing bar association rules on the unauthorized practice of law (“UPL”). The intersection of large language models with UPL raises manifold issues, including those pertaining to important and developing jurisprudence on free speech, antitrust, occupational licensing, and the inherent-powers doctrine. How the intersection is navigated, moreover, is of vital importance in the durative struggle for access to justice, and low-income individuals will be disproportionately impacted. In this Article, we offer a recommendation that is both attuned to technological advances and avoids the extremes that have characterized the past decades of the UPL debate. Rather than abandon UPL rules, and rather than leave them undisturbed, we propose that they be recast as primarily regulation of entity-type claims. Through this recasting, bar associations can retain their role as the ultimate determiners of “lawyer” and “attorney” classifications while allowing nonlawyers, including the AI-powered entities that have emerged in recent years, to provide legal services—save for a narrow and clearly defined subset. Although this recommendation is novel, it is easy to implement, comes with few downsides, and would further the twin UPL aims of competency and ethicality better than traditional UPL enforcement. Legal technology companies would be freed from operating in a legal gray area; states would no longer have to create elaborate UPL- avoiding mechanisms, such as Utah’s “legal sandbox”; consumers—both individuals and companies—would benefit from better and cheaper legal services; and the dismantling of access-to-justice barriers would finally be possible. Moreover, the clouds of free speech and antitrust challenges that are massing above current UPL rules would dissipate, and bar associations would be able to focus on fulfilling their already established UPL-related aims.

## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.5250633`

- title: Physics of Language Models: Part 3.1, Knowledge Storage and Extraction
- authors: Zeyuan Allen-Zhu, Yuanzhi Li
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2025-05-14
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.5250633
- keyword hits: fine-tuning, large language model, large language models, llm, llms, token embedding

### abstract

Large language models (LLMs) can store a vast amount of world knowledge, often extractable via question-answering (e.g., "What is Abraham Lincoln's birthday?''). However, do they answer such questions based on exposure to similar questions during training (i.e., cheating), or by genuinely learning to extract knowledge from sources like Wikipedia? In this paper, we investigate this issue using a controlled biography dataset. We find a strong correlation between the model's ability to extract knowledge and various diversity measures of the training data. Essentially , for knowledge to be reliably extracted, it must be sufficiently augmented (e.g., through paraphrasing, sentence shuffling, translations) during pretraining . Without such augmentation, knowledge may be memorized but not extractable, leading to 0% accuracy, regardless of subsequent instruction fine-tuning. To understand why this occurs, we employ (nearly) linear probing to demonstrate a strong connection between the observed correlation and how the model internally encodes knowledge --- whether it is linearly encoded in the hidden embeddings of entity names or distributed across other token embeddings in the training text. This paper provides several key recommendations for LLM pretraining in the industry: (1) rewrite the pretraining data --- using small, auxiliary models --- to provide knowledge augmentation, and (2) incorporate more instruction-finetuning data into the pretraining stage before it becomes too late.

This paper introduces LLM SEO Files , a novel conceptual framework designed to optimize digital content for visibility, retrievability, and citation across AI-powered search platforms, including ChatGPT, Google AI Overview and Perplexity. While traditional SEO and emerging paradigms such as Answer Engine Optimization (AEO) or Generative Engine Optimization (GEO) have adapted to shifting search behaviors, a critical gap remains: the absence of a systematic, file-based methodology aligned with how large language models (LLMs) parse, interpret, and attribute information. To address this, we propose a six-layer symbolic framework comprising: TXT files (llms.txt), YAML configuration (index.yaml), Markdown summaries (page-name.md), Schema.org markup (schema.JSON-LD), and a newly introduced json file for the Evidence of Facts ( evidence-of-facts.json ). ### abstract

This paper explores the challenges of adapting intellectual property frameworks for the era of large language models (LLMs), with a focus on data sovereignty, community rights, and equitable benefit distribution. We examine the limitations of current approaches, such as fair use doctrine and Creative Commons licenses, which often fail to capture the nuanced, relational social norms that govern knowledge and data within communities, particularly those with low-resource languages or indigenous backgrounds. We propose a generative framework, Language Model Input for Data & Society (LIDS), a direct response to these challenges. Designed eith principles from evolutionary economics, indigenous data sovereignty and participatory design, LIDS is designed to be compatible with more community-centric licensing architectures. Our goal is to suggest a potential direction for developing agreements that might better operationalize complex social values and incentives. The legal-technical infrastructures of LIDS recognizes licensing of data as a collective and personal structure, while acknowledging its significant technical and social foundations and challenges.

## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.5403965`

- title: Benchmarking Islamic Awareness: HUMAINChat, ChatGPT, and the AIBIA Framework
- authors: Aadil Bouhlaoui
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2025-09-08
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.5403965
- keyword hits: chatgpt, foundation model, gemini, llama

### abstract

In August 2025, Saudi Arabia’s national AI company HUMAIN announced the release of its Arabic-first foundation model, ALLaM 34B, trained on proprietary, in-Kingdom datasets and deployed via the HUMAIN Chat application. Positioned as a culturally and religiously aligned alternative to Western models, ALLaM reflects broader national ambitions under Vision 2030 to localize AI development and assert digital sovereignty. These claims raise a fundamental question for both AI and policy communities: how can Islamic Awareness—a model’s ability to engage credibly and responsibly with Islamic texts, law, and ethics—be rigorously defined and measured? This paper introduces AIBIA (AI Islamic Awareness Benchmark), a novel evaluation framework designed to assess language models on their handling of Islamic knowledge and ethical reasoning. AIBIA structures its evaluation pipeline along the continuum of traditional Islamic pedagogy (Madrasa → Ḥalaqah → Jāmiʿa → Mashyakha), mapping tasks across domains such as Qur’ān recitation, ḥadīth sciences, fiqh, uṣūl al-fiqh, comparative madhāhib, and fiqh al-aqalliyyāt. The methodology integrates model self-assessment, cross-model peer review, and expert human adjudication. It draws on contemporary research in AI alignment, which has shown that language models can approximate human judgments while still exhibiting significant bias, limitations in doctrinal understanding, and safety vulnerabilities. AIBIA offers a transparent and auditable framework for Islamic awareness evaluation, accommodating both doctrinal plurality and red-team safety protocols. It is compatible with cloud-hosted APIs (e.g., OpenAI, Gemini, Anthropic) and no-auth local deployments (e.g., LM Studio, Ollama via OpenRouter). The benchmark incorporates safety checks to detect hallucinations and mitigate risks associated with extremist-adjacent prompts. A preliminary comparative test between HUMAINChat and ChatGPT—analysed by Gemini 2.5—demonstrates the need for structured evaluation, as ChatGPT outperformed HUMAINChat in specificity, interactivity, and citation depth despite the latter’s culturally targeted training. As Arabic-first models like ALLaM transition from research to public deployment, culturally grounded benchmarks like AIBIA will be critical in evaluating whether such models meet the standards of religious competence, epistemic safety, and ethical responsibility required in sovereign AI contexts.
