## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.7243715`

- title: Engineering a Lightweight, Cross-Generator-Robust Pipeline forBengali AI-Paraphrase Detection: A Leakage- and Artifact-Aware Evaluation Process
- authors: Rahat  Ahmed Jobu
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-06
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7243715
- keyword hits: gpt-3, instruction-tuned, llm, qwen

### abstract

Deploying an AI-generated-text detector in production is an engineering trade-off: fine-tuned transformers reach the highest accuracy but need GPU inference, while lightweight, CPU-only classifiers are cheap to deploy but their reliability under real-world paraphrasing is largely unverified. This paper stress-tests a lightweight detection pipeline for Bengali, spoken by over 230 million people, whose existing detectors have only been evaluated against one paraphrasing method (GPT-3.5). A benchmark of 600 human-written Bengali sentences was paraphrased using two mechanisms — a direct instruction-tuned LLM (Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct) and back-translation (NLLB-200) through two pivot languages — yielding three generator instances, two sharing a model. A BanglaBERT-base transformer and three lightweight classifiers (logistic regression, linear SVM, XGBoost) were trained under a leakage-free protocol and evaluated on same- and cross-generator sets. A SHAP audit of XGBoost surfaced spurious tokens traced to a numeral-rendering artifact; after correction and five-seed re-running, the reliability gap proved model-dependent: BanglaBERT-base retained a sizable gap (7.4 points, n=3), logistic regression a small gap supported by a t-test (1.9 points, p=0.011, though seed non-independence makes this suggestive) but not by Wilcoxon (p=0.0625), while linear SVM (p=0.27) and XGBoost (p=0.90) showed no reliable gap post-correction. An inference-cost benchmark shows the lightweight pipeline costs $0.00003–$0.000045 per 1,000 requests — roughly 15–21× cheaper than a realistically GPU-served transformer under matched serving conditions — at the cost of a 10–14 point accuracy gap and cross-generator recall of only 0.45–0.57 across all models. Results demonstrate that a pipeline's reliability and cost advantages must be verified empirically, not assumed, before deployment.

- title: Structural Validation of LLM-Generated Microservice Decompositions Using Source-Code Dependencies
- authors: Daniel Silva, José Renan, Emanuel  Dantas Filho, Ademar Fran&ccedil;a de Sousa Neto, Mirko Perkusich, Danyllo  Wagner Albuquerque, Kyller Gorgônio, Angelo Perkusich
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-08-07
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7206905
- keyword hits: large language model, large language models, llm, llms, prompting

### abstract

Decomposing monolithic systems into microservices is a key activity in software modernization. Although Large Language Models (LLMs) can generate semantically plausible decompositions from textual requirements, it remains unclear whether these proposals preserve the structural dependencies implemented in the source code. This paper evaluates the structural adherence of microservice decompositions generated by OpenAI o3 for the PetClinic and Bookstore systems. We propose an automated validation pipeline based on static dependency analysis and compare zero-shot and few-shot prompting using dependency preservation (TPD) and dependency violation (TVD) metrics. A robustness analysis was conducted to control for differences in class-to-service mapping coverage. After normalization, both prompting strategies produced equivalent structural adherence, achieving TPD values of 68.0% (PetClinic) and 83.3% (Bookstore). The findings demonstrate that structural evaluations of LLM-generated decompositions should explicitly control for mapping coverage, as apparent differences between prompting strategies may otherwise reflect methodological bias rather than genuine architectural quality.

- title: Designing and Evaluating an Agentic AI Pipeline for Microservice Architecture Generation from User Stories
- authors: Daniel Silva, Danyllo  Wagner Albuquerque, Mirko Perkusich, Guillermo Rodríguez, Jorge  Andrés Diaz-Pace, Kyller Gorgônio, Angelo Perkusich
- affiliations: not stated

- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7207018
- keyword hits: agentic, large language model, large language models, llm, llms

### abstract

Large Language Models (LLMs) have shown promising capabilities for generating software architectures directly from natural language requirements. However, most existing approaches rely on a single LLM, providing limited support for collaborative architectural reasoning and validation. This study investigates the feasibility of an Agentic AI workflow for generating microservice architectures from user stories. We propose a multi-agent pipeline comprising two software architect agents with complementary design perspectives and a validator agent responsible for consolidating candidate architectures through different collaboration strategies. The approach was evaluated on two benchmark systems (BookStore and Pet-Clinic) by comparing the generated architectures against reference decompositions in terms of service identification and inter-service communication links. The proposed workflow achieved F1-scores of up to 1.00 for service identification and 0.88 for interaction recovery. Although the multi-agent workflow achieved performance comparable to a strong Few-Shot baseline, the results indicate that its main benefit lies in providing a structured and extensible architectural reasoning process through collaborative validation rather than consistently improving decomposition accuracy.

- title: SmartNorms4BIM: Automated Code Compliance based on Semantic Reasoning
- authors: Ignacio Huitzil, Lucía Pitarch, Marco Schorlemmer, Nardine Osman, Pep Coll, Fernando Bobillo
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-03-02
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.6329621
- keyword hits: large language model, large language models, llm, llms

### abstract

Automatising the process of verifying whether the design of a building conforms to a certain regulation is an important task to save time and money. SmartNorms4BIM is a novel building regulatory compliance-checking system that facilitates the automatic evaluation of Building Information Modelling (BIM) models. The system's architecture follows Eastman's four stages: translation of norms into a machine-readable format, BIM model preparation, execution of the verification process, and report generation. Our proposal uses Semantic Web technologies (OWL2 ontologies and semantic reasoners) to represent the knowledge and detect violations of the norms automatically, Large Language Models (LLMs) to interpret and transform building norms into OWL 2 ontologies, fuzzy logic to manage vagueness in the norms, and specific algorithms to reason with geometric knowledge. A Java desktop prototype was developed to determine whether a BIM model complies with a given set of norms. To validate our approach, two real-world use cases were studied.

## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.6638918`

- title: The Mirror Problem: Ai Bias As Reflected Cognition
- authors: Daniel Ziekenoppasser-Powell
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-05-06
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.6638918
- keyword hits: large language model, large language models, llm

### abstract

Current AI governance treats bias as a technical defect amenable to algorithmic correction. This paper argues that bias in Large Language Models is a structural reflection of human cognitive patterns, faithfully reproduced from training data encoding decades of human text and communication. The claim is behavioural equivalence, not mechanistic equivalence: LLM outputs exhibit the same bias patterns as their human sources, and for governance purposes the downstream effects are what matter. The paper presents a systematic mapping of human cognitive biases to LLM behaviours and proposes a two-category predictive criterion distinguishing externalised biases (embedded in text, readily transferred) from embodied biases (requiring lived experience, hypothesised not to transfer). Taking RLHF as a case study, it argues that alignment is shallow relative to pretraining distributions. Effective governance requires a shift from pre-deployment certification to continuous post-deployment monitoring, and a psychotechnological discipline bridging cognitive science and AI governance.

## uid: `doi:10.2139/ssrn.6740799`

- title: AI AS A THIRD-PARTY CONSULTANT? THE CORPORATE ATTORNEY-CLIENT PRIVILEGE & WORK PRODUCT DOCTRINE APPLIED TO GENERATIVE AI
- authors: Michele DeStefano
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-05-09
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.6740799
- keyword hits: chatgpt, claude, generative ai

### abstract

Lawyers routinely rely on generative AI platforms--both general-purpose tools such as Claude and ChatGPT and legally focused platforms such as Harvey, Legora, and CoCounsel--to analyze transactions, assess risk, draft documents, and structure legal advice for corporate clients. Currently, however, there is no agreement among federal courts on whether the attorney-client privilege or work product doctrine protects these communications. Four landmark 2026 decisions frame the debate. United States v. Heppner (S.D.N.Y. Feb. 17, 2026) held that documents a criminal defendant generated using a consumer AI platform were not privileged or protected as work product. Warner v. Gilbarco, Inc. (E.D. Mich. Feb. 10, 2026) reached the opposite conclusion, holding that AI-assisted litigation materials constitute protected work product and that treating AI tools as third parties for waiver purposes would "nullify work-product protection in nearly every modern drafting environment." Morgan v. V2X, Inc. (D. Colo. Mar. 30, 2026) substantially extended Warner, analogizing AI to email, phones, and smart devices, and introduced AI-specific protective order language. And the Sixth Circuit in United States v. Farris (Apr. 3, 2026) issued ethical guidance emphasizing safeguarding privilege when attorneys use AI. The critical unresolved question is how courts will treat attorney-directed use of closed, proprietary AI systems that contractually prohibit vendor access to client inputs. This Article makes three main arguments. First, the work product doctrine, although more easily applied to AI, is insufficient because it applies only in anticipation of litigation. Much of the most consequential AI-assisted legal work--M&A due diligence, regulatory compliance, contract drafting--occurs in transactional contexts where work product offers no protection. This limitation makes essential the privilege recommendations this Article proposes. Second, it argues that waiver analysis related to use of AI should be similar to that applied to other third-party vendors like e-discovery vendors, or contract analytics tools used by lawyers. As long as there is a reasonable expectation of confidentiality, Federal courts have never held that uploading privileged materials to contract analytics software, e-discovery platforms, or legal research tools constitutes a third-party disclosure that waives privilege or work product protection. Third, this Article argues that AI platforms should be analyzed under the same doctrine applied to human third-party consultants. AI platforms are functionally the most capable consultants in the history of the legal profession. The question is not whether they are "real" third parties in a philosophical sense, but whether they perform functions that, when performed by humans, courts have protected or declined to protect. However, applying that framework reveals that the emerging doctrine is at once potentially too narrow and too broad. Under the narrowest interpretation, use of AI would be categorically excluded from the privilege framework even when the substantive conditions for privilege—attorney direction, legal purpose, maintained confidentiality— are fully satisfied. Under the broadest application, courts might protect all inputs to, outputs from, and communications with a closed AI platform. This is because when AI is involved, the “legal purpose” inquiry becomes so easy to satisfy on the attorney's side and extremely hard to successfully challenge on the opponent's side, particularly in circuits applying the significant purpose test. This creates a de facto zone of secrecy even though the doctrine formally requires legal purpose, a problem amplified by the unresolved circuit split between the primary purpose and significant purpose tests left intact by the Supreme Court's dismissal of In re Grand Jury as improvidently granted. This Article proposes analysis under a single, uniform agency-based standard requiring a strong nexus between the AI's function and the legal advice ultimately provided, assessed through four factors: (1) who directed the AI use; (2) the nature of the platform--enterprise and closed, or consumer and open; (3) what is being sought to be protected--an output, input, prompt, or communication exchange; and (4) the purpose of the use, specifically whether providing legal advice was a primary or significant purpose. Unlike a categorical rule, this multi-factored nexus test embraces the essential role AI plays in the provision of high-quality legal advice while preventing corporations from using AI-laundered communications as a discovery shield.

- title: Emergent Non-Classical Probabilistic Structure in Large Language Models under Contextual Perturbations
- authors: Jyotiranjan Beuria
- affiliations: not stated
- posted: 2026-07-29
- source: SSRN
- link: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.6504379
- keyword hits: large language model, large language models, llm, llms

### abstract

We investigate the probabilistic structure of responses generated by large language models (LLMs) under varying contextual prompts. Interpreting model outputs as observable quantities of an underlying high-dimensional system, we examine whether they admit a classical probabilistic representation. Controlled experiments reveal systematic violations of the law of total probability together with pronounced order-dependent effects. We show that these deviations are not arbitrary but exhibit a structured interference form, characterized by a phase-dependent term and a non-trivial upper bound. The observed scaling relation and strict adherence to this bound indicate that the resulting probability assignments cannot be embedded within a single Kolmogorov space, but instead reflect a constrained non-commutative structure. These findings highlight empirical evidence that LLMs may be viewed as complex systems whose contextual responses give rise to emergent non-classical probabilistic behavior.
